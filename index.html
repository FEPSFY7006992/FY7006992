Abstract

    Modern petrol performance engines rely on turbocharging to maximise power output from smaller displacements. 
    However, fixed geometry turbochargers (FGTs) compromise on performance due to their fixed proportions. 
    This paper examines Variable Geometry Turbocharging (VGT), specifically Variable Nozzle Turbochargers (VNTs) in petrol applications. 
    These turbochargers allow the internal dimensions of the turbocharger to change to improve performance and throttle response across 
    the RPM range of the engine.

Introduction

    Modern performance engines are expected to maximise power output from ever smaller displacements (total volume of all engine cylinders) 
    in the name of efficiency. A common technique implemented by leading vehicle manufacturers to attain impressive power to displacement 
    ratios is to use forced induction. The most common method of forced induction amongst small displacement petrol engines is turbocharging, 
    where exhaust gases from the engine are used to drive a compressor which compresses intake air to create a denser intake air mix. This 
    allows more oxygen to be present in the same volume inside the cylinder, allowing more fuel to be burned and therefore more power to be 
    produced from the engine.  

 

    Traditional turbocharging methods are not perfect though. It is impossible to optimise the shape of a fixed geometry system to be 
    efficient at all rev ranges as the volume of exhaust gas passing through is variable and this introduces a problem known as turbo lag. 
    This is defined as “the poor transient response of the turbocharger at low engine loads” (Feneley et al. (2017)). This means that the 
    smaller volume of air passing through the turbocharger at low engine speeds is insufficient to spin the compressor fast enough for serious 
    power gains in oversized turbos, or the volume moves the compressor quickly at low engine speeds but chokes the flow at higher engine speeds 
    in undersized housings. This causes a change in how quickly the engine responds to throttle input at different engine speeds. This is not 
    a desirable quality in performance engines but can be rectified with the use of variable geometry turbocharging.

Analysis and discussion

    Variable Nozzle Turbochargers 

    Variable geometry turbochargers (VGT) allow for the aspect ratio (the internal width of the volute/housing) of the turbocharger to be manipulated 
    (Feneley et al. (2017)) in ways that traditional turbochargers simply do not allow for. In the case of petrol performance engines, the most common 
    type of VGT is the Variable Nozzle Turbocharger (VNT). Aspect ratio variance is accomplished using moveable aerodynamic guide vanes surrounding the 
    turbine wheel (illustrated in Fig.1). At low engine speeds, an actuating mechanism keeps the vanes nearly closed which creates narrow nozzles. This 
    allows for smaller volumes of exhaust gases to still provide enough kinetic energy over the turbine wheel to efficiently spool the intake compressor,
    similar to how you can cover the end of a hose with your thumb to increase the pressure of the water exiting the hose.

    
    Fig. 1 Source: (Feneley et al. (2017)) 

    Once engine RPMs (Revolutions Per Minute) increase, and the volume of exhaust gases passing through the volute increases, the actuating mechanism 
    (usually consisting of a vane control ring that can be controlled pneumatically with a diaphragm actuator, hydraulically via engine oil pressure or 
    electronically through a stepper motor and ECU (Engine Control Unit) signals (Feneley et al. (2017)) opens the vanes to increase the aspect ratio and allow 
    for the greater volume of gas to provide this kinetic energy instead. Allowing the greater volume of exhaust gas to pass is important as it reduces 
    exhaust backpressure at higher engine speeds, meaning the pistons have to do less work to expel larger exhaust volumes through the otherwise same size 
    nozzle. This allows for the kinetic energy being provided to the turbine wheel to be kept much more consistent throughout the entire rev range compared 
    with a traditional fixed geometry turbocharger. This results in a more consistent compression of intake air at all engine speeds and therefore leads to 
    better throttle response across the rev range. Technologies such as this require special materials such as Titanium Silicon Carbide, Stainless Steel 353 MA 
    or Inconel 751 (Shettigar et al. (2014)) to handle the high exhaust temperatures of a petrol engine (>1000°C) and therefore these advancements first came 
    to market only recently in car development in the 2006 Porsche 911 Turbo S (Tang et al. (2014)). 

    Comparison to Fixed Geometry Turbocharging 

    This change in aspect ratio is particularly useful when comparing variable technologies to fixed geometry turbocharging. Taking the Variable Flow turbocharger 
    (VFT) as an example (As it changes the aspect ratio in a similar way to a VNT, just with a different mechanism), the variable aspect ratio allows the VFT to 
    combine both the strengths of the small volute FGT aspect ratio in the lower RPM range and the large volute FGT aspect ratio in the upper RPM range order to 
    create the most effective charging solution across the whole RPM range of the engine (shown in Figure. 2).

    
    Fig. 2 Source: (Dasgupta et al. (2020)) 

    This results in a wider torque plateau across its entire RPM range and also indicates that the turbocharger is spooling effectively at a greater range of  
    RPM than the Fixed geometry turbocharger (FGT). Having this torque available across the whole spectrum of RPM like this means a reduced boost threshold, as 
    the engine reaches a boost plateau faster (at a lower RPM) and therefore reduces the feeling of a ‘turbo lag’ like you would traditionally feel with a FGT. 
    Taking this graph as an example, if your engine has an RPM limit of 6500RPM and a large volute FGT, you may only start to feel the effect of the boost around 
    3000RPM and throttle response below 3000RPM would be less sharp. However, with a VFT fitted, this extra torque is made available starting around 1600RPM, which 
    means that the turbocharger is working effectively from just above idle speed (The RPM at which the engine rests when no throttle is applied) to the RPM limit, 
    giving better throttle response across the entire RPM range of the engine. This works in the same way for VGTs.
